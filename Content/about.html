This is a loosely curated collection of images, largely documenting the mud dome, alongside a range of related—and occasionally unrelated—experiments and side projects involving the house itself, a timber California Bungalow, as well as ceramics, lighting, finishes, and whatever has become the latest obsession.

The website began with a relatively simple idea: to create a timeline of images driven by a Google Sheet. That quickly spiralled into a broader experiment exploring how many different ways a single collection of images could be represented, organised, and experienced. Questions emerged around layouts, timelines, interfaces, colour systems, typography, and eventually the construction of an entire backend system to control the site. In some ways, the process mirrors the mud dome itself: what began as making a few mud bricks gradually evolved into the much larger and stranger project it is now.

This is largely a weekend and holiday activity and sits somewhat in opposition to the everyday practice of architecture, with its programmes, deadlines, critical paths, consultants and deliverables. Yet some things remain familiar. Budget, for example, is a constant constraint. As with any project, more budget usually means more ambition and perhaps greater scope for risk-taking. There are moments where I would prefer not to be digging 1100mm-deep footings by hand—particularly having already done some damage in the process. Employing someone younger and fitter occasionally seems like a sensible architectural decision.

The dome itself is less a finished object and more a vehicle for testing ideas. These include different approaches to concrete formwork, both traditional and 3D printed, integrating custom 3D-printed lighting directly into concrete, and learning by doing: how to build with mud, make bricks, and render with lime.

Alongside this are ongoing material experiments, particularly involving clay and casting. This includes developing slip-casting processes using clay sourced directly from the site, working through particle size, mixing ratios, deflocculants and consistency testing with hydrometers. While technical in nature, these processes have largely been developed through trial, error and iteration rather than within any formal setup.

The project itself began during COVID, during a period of working alone from home. It started with excavating beneath a veranda and making mud bricks. The first batch turned out to be entirely the wrong shape and size, requiring the process to begin again. Those original bricks remain stacked on pallets waiting for another purpose—possibly a kiln one day.

More broadly, the house acts as a kind of laboratory for ideas; some more resolved than others, and some that should almost certainly never be tested on client projects. There is no particular ambition for it ever to be considered finished.

The website follows the same thinking. It is not intended to be polished or fixed, but instead acts as an ongoing experiment exploring how the same body of work can be organised and viewed through different systems and interfaces. It also reflects a broader idea: that most things can be learned by doing.

I do not come from a background in web development or coding. This website is written directly in HTML and JavaScript without templates or software layers. Alongside this I have written bits of Python and VB where required for other applications and workflows. AI has not replaced something I previously did, nor simply made it easier. In many respects, it has made some of this possible in the first place. This feels less like a dumbing down and more like a removal of barriers; without it I probably would not have invested the time—or had the time—to attempt much of it at all.

That is the project as it currently stands.
